ABSTRACT — REPLACEMENT

JangDi is a web-based micro-task platform developed for the Mae Fah Luang University student community. The project organizes short, local service tasks through a shared workflow for hirers, workers, and administrators. Hirers can publish jobs and select applicants, while workers can advertise services and accept service requests. Both entry points lead to a common process for assignment, payment-state tracking, completion evidence, and confirmation. The application uses Vue 3 for the client, Node.js and Express for the server, and MongoDB for persistence. Authentication supports the designated university email domain, and a member can switch between hirer and worker activities within one account. Location-based notifications use stored coordinates and a configurable proximity radius. Administrative functions support post approval, user management, reports, SOS submissions, payment review, and tester feedback. Public profiles are separated from restricted contact and bank information. Stripe test mode and prototype payment records support development and testing without establishing a live financial settlement service. The report describes the implemented design and provides a structure for documenting functional verification. Results from a formal evaluation with actual testers remain to be added.

CHAPTER 1 — INTRODUCTION

1.1 Background and Rationale

Students may need assistance with short tasks such as collecting items, delivering documents, and providing local services. Within the JangDi project context, the central problem is coordinating a request with an available participant and maintaining a clear record of the agreement. The application is designed to make the task description, selected worker, payment status, and completion status available within a shared workflow.

A posting channel alone does not determine how applicants are selected, when work is ready to begin, or what happens when a participant requests a cancellation or refund. JangDi addresses these coordination requirements through explicit job states, role-based actions, and administrative review. Its intended community uses the designated Mae Fah Luang University email domain, and one member account supports both requesting and providing services.

The project provides two ways to initiate work. A hirer can publish a job and choose a worker from the applicants. Alternatively, a worker can advertise a service, receive a request, and decide whether to accept it. These paths share the later job workflow so that payment records, completion evidence, reviews, and exceptional cases can be associated with the same work record. The intended benefits motivate the design; their extent must be established through evaluation rather than assumed from implementation.

1.3 Scope

1.3.1 Member Authentication and Profiles

The system supports registration and sign-in using the designated @lamduan.mfu.ac.th email domain. Google sign-in and password-based access refer to a shared account when they use the same normalized email address. Email verification and password recovery support account access. New-member onboarding collects profile, contact, and bank-account fields. During prototype testing, the interface permits explicitly identified dummy bank information; these entries are not verified bank accounts.

Members can upload an avatar, maintain profile information, and switch between hirer and worker interfaces. Other users can open an appropriate read-only profile. Public fields are separated from private information, and contact email is available only to the owner, administrators, and authorized job counterparts after selection, including completed work. Email-domain access does not independently verify current student enrollment through university records.

1.3.2 Hirer Functions

Hirers can create job posts, provide the required task information, and optionally add supplementary notes. Submitted posts are subject to content checks and administrator review. Hirers can inspect applicants, select a worker, initiate the available prototype payment process, follow progress, inspect submitted completion evidence, and confirm completion. They can also request advertised services, submit applicable refund requests, review their work history, and provide job-related feedback.

1.3.3 Worker Functions

Workers can browse available jobs, inspect task details and permitted profile information, and apply for suitable work. They can advertise services with availability periods and capacity settings, and accept or decline incoming service requests. Accepted requests create linked jobs. Workers can follow assigned work, start work when the payment conditions are satisfied, submit completion evidence, inspect transaction records, and review the hirer where permitted.

1.3.4 Administrator Functions

Administrators can review job and service posts, manage user-account status, inspect reports, and handle SOS records. Financial administration includes inspection of payment records, payout evidence, and refund requests within the prototype workflow. Administrators can also inspect tester comments and distinguish new comments from those already read. Administrative access is enforced by server-side authorization.

1.3.5 System Boundaries

The prototype uses location coordinates for proximity-related features and a configurable nearby-notification radius, with a default of approximately two kilometers. Stored coordinates are not continuous GPS tracking. The intended campus context must be distinguished from a technically enforced geographic boundary; the current report must not claim an implemented campus-wide five-kilometer geofence without evidence of that restriction.

Payments use Stripe test mode or the supported demonstration/evidence-based workflow. Application states such as held, payout_pending, and released describe prototype records and do not establish live bank settlement or a licensed escrow service. Notification timing, usability improvements, security effectiveness, and financial outcomes are not quantified without measured evidence. The current implementation does not include history-based AI matching or an interactive Google Maps pin-selection interface.

Design specifies the interface structure, application architecture, and data models. Implementation integrates the client, server, database, identity service, and test-payment provider. Verification examines the implemented behavior against defined cases; user evaluation is documented separately when it has been conducted.

2.1 Related Theory

Two-sided platform theory considers services that facilitate interaction between different participant groups [1]. JangDi applies this perspective to hirers and workers, whose activities are different but depend on shared task information. The design separates their available actions while allowing a member to participate on either side through one account. This is a design rationale rather than evidence of market growth or network effects.

A workflow model represents work as states and permitted transitions. JangDi distinguishes post approval, applications, assignment, work execution, completion confirmation, and exceptional outcomes. Payment processing is represented separately because financial review and task execution do not always advance together. This separation helps the application determine which actions are appropriate for the current record.

The design also separates public identity information from information needed only by the parties performing a job. Ratings, completion evidence, and administrative reports provide records associated with platform activities. These mechanisms are intended to support accountability; their effectiveness in reducing misconduct remains an evaluation question.

2.2 Related Research

Horton and Chilton examine labor supply and monetary incentives in paid crowdsourcing [2]. Their work concerns online tasks and cannot directly establish student behavior in local service work. For JangDi, it supports considering compensation and task requirements as explicit information while recognizing that physical location and practical coordination introduce additional design requirements.

Ba and Pavlou examine trust-building technology and buyer behavior in electronic markets [3]. Their findings motivate attention to feedback and transaction context. JangDi’s job-linked reviews and completion records are implementation choices informed by that concern. The project does not infer that the same measured effects automatically apply to its users.

3.2 New System

The proposed-and-implemented system provides a common account model with hirer and worker activities and a separate administrator role. Hirer-created jobs use an application-and-selection process. Worker-created service advertisements use requests that are accepted or declined by the worker. Accepted requests produce linked jobs, after which both paths share payment-state checks, work execution, completion evidence, and confirmation.

The browser communicates with the Express API for authenticated actions and record retrieval. Server-side rules control access and state changes, and Mongoose models persist records in MongoDB. External identity, email, and test-payment services support specific operations. Socket.IO supplements stored notifications for connected clients. A redirect URL or a client-side button state is not treated as an authoritative payment or permission decision.

3.7 Database Design

3.7.1 Logical Document Relationships

The implementation uses MongoDB documents represented by Mongoose schemas. ObjectId fields reference related records, while selected structures such as bank information and request details are embedded. These references are application-level relationships rather than SQL foreign-key constraints. The updated diagram should therefore identify document references, embedded objects, unique indexes, and geospatial indexes without describing the database as relational SQL storage.

ServicePost | Worker advertisement, availability, location, capacity, and post status
ServiceRequest | Hirer request linked to a service post and, after acceptance, its resulting job

A User can create many Jobs and ServicePosts. A Job can receive multiple JobWaiting applications and references its selected worker separately. A ServicePost can receive many ServiceRequests. An accepted ServiceRequest can be associated with one resulting Job, and a partial unique index prevents multiple Jobs from referencing the same non-null origin request. A Payment references one Job through a unique job field. Feedback references the job, author, and recipient, with a unique job-direction combination. Notifications, reports, SOS records, and logs retain the relevant actor and record relationships.

CHAPTER 5 — CONCLUSION

5.1 Conclusion

This project developed JangDi, a university-oriented micro-task web application with hirer, worker, and administrator roles. The implementation supports hirer-created jobs with applicant selection and worker-created service advertisements with incoming requests. Accepted service requests connect to the same job process used for assigned hirer-created work. Payment-state tracking, completion evidence, and confirmation provide records for the later stages of that process.

The supporting implementation includes institutional email authentication, password recovery, role switching, profile images, restricted contact disclosure, persisted notifications, and administrative management tools. Tester comments are maintained separately from job reviews. The system uses Vue 3, Node.js and Express, MongoDB, and supporting identity, notification, email, and test-payment integrations.

The current payment workflow remains a prototype using Stripe test mode and applicable demonstration records. Functional verification must be documented with recorded outcomes, and conclusions about user experience depend on evaluation with actual participants. The presence of the implemented features does not by itself establish increased income, improved trust, or reduced task-completion time.

[After completing Chapter 4, add a short paragraph stating exactly which tested objectives were met, which issues remain, and the supported findings from testers. Avoid “all requirements were fulfilled” unless the scope checklist and evidence support it.]

5.2 Problems and Limitations

The design requires consistency between two ways of initiating work and the shared records used afterward. An accepted service request must remain associated with the correct job, while capacity and availability conditions can change. The report should discuss the actual problems and fixes observed during development rather than treating the two workflows as independent features.

Navigation to an external payment provider introduces cases where a return page has limited or unhelpful internal browser history. Payment confirmation can also arrive through server events separately from the browser return. The interface and server therefore require consistent state retrieval and explicit return destinations. Prototype payment records must remain distinguishable from live settlement.

Profile information must be presented consistently across the owner’s view, other members’ views, and administrator views. Contact disclosure depends on the viewer’s relationship to the job, while bank information must remain outside public profile responses. Notification usefulness also depends on location availability, connection state, and stored coordinates. An outdated stored location does not establish a user’s current physical position.

The current documentation has no completed formal user-evaluation dataset. Consequently, the report cannot yet quantify usability, trust, notification latency, or production reliability. Dummy bank information, test payments, and temporary test access further limit what can be inferred about a production deployment.

[Confirm which development problems the team actually encountered and add dated records or issue references where available. Do not describe every potential limitation as an observed incident.]

5.3 Future Work

The first priority is to execute the prepared functional cases and conduct the agreed evaluation with actual participants. Findings should guide improvements to forms, status explanations, navigation, and error recovery. Further technical work may include more precise location selection, assessment of notification delivery and location freshness, and additional safeguards for operational review.

A production payment service would require a separate decision about settlement, payout responsibilities, provider configuration, and applicable operating requirements. Dummy testing information would need to be separated from verified operational data. Expansion to other communities would also require revisiting identity, privacy, and moderation policies. History-based recommendations or continuous location tracking should remain proposed extensions unless they are implemented and evaluated with an appropriate data and privacy design.
